# Leave the Click

Dear Maya,

I found Father's radio behind the winter blankets yesterday, wrapped in the towel with the blue border. You asked me to send photographs before I cleared the house, but this seemed to deserve a letter. In a photograph it would look like an ordinary wooden box, one corner darkened by a cup that nobody admitted leaving there. You would not see the small delay between turning the tuning knob and hearing a voice. That pause was part of our evenings. Father used it to pull out a chair, and Mother used it to ask whether anyone wanted more tea.

The radio came with a service manual, which I found in the bottom drawer beneath a bundle of old receipts. Father had written dates in the margins and drawn a careful circle around one screw. I used to think these marks were instructions for making things last forever. Now they look more like a record of his willingness to begin again. He could spend an entire Saturday tracing a fault, then accept an imperfect result without disappointment. Even after a repair, a little static remained between the stations. He never called that failure; he simply turned the volume down.

When I turned the tuning knob this morning, the familiar click brought back not a station but the narrow kitchen, where you stood on a chair to reach the shelf and I pretended I was tall enough already. I remembered your sleeve brushing the bread, the wet windows, and the way we lowered our voices when the weather report began. I cannot remember the forecast itself. Perhaps memory is less interested in keeping information than in keeping company. The click lasted only a moment, but for that moment I did not feel as though I was sorting the house alone.

I took the radio to the repair shop near the bridge. The woman at the counter asked whether I wanted it to look new. She could replace the cloth over the speaker and polish away the pale ring on the wood. I heard myself say, "Make it safe, but leave the click." She smiled and wrote that on the job card without asking me to explain. I left the service manual with her too. There are practical reasons to keep old notes, even when the person who made them can no longer answer questions. Not every mark is damage, and not every improvement is a kindness.

Before you worry, I am not turning the house into a museum. I gave the cracked bowls to the neighbour who grows herbs, and I let the delivery driver take the table we both disliked. Keeping everything would make it difficult to live here; keeping nothing would make leaving seem easier than it is. The radio is small enough to stand by my window. I want to hear unfamiliar voices through it, along with the static, and let new evenings gather around the old ones. An object can carry a memory without being asked to carry our whole past.

When you visit next month, bring the cassette you recorded at the seaside, if you can find it. I know the radio cannot play it. We can borrow a player and listen to the two machines in turn, giving each its own place on the table. You can turn the tuning knob while I make tea. We may disagree about which station Father preferred; we always did. I do not need the radio to settle that question. I only want something we can sit beside while we tell our different versions, and a little silence when neither of us has anything more to say. There is still room in that silence for us.

With love,
Daniel
